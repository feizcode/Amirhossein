# Grade 5 Mathematics — English Knowledge Guide

**Source:** `Books/ریاضی.pdf`, the 151-page Persian fifth-grade mathematics textbook.
**Scope:** English rendering of the book's chapter structure, core explanations, methods, and learning objectives. The many illustrated classroom activities and exercise sets are described by skill rather than reproduced question by question. Printed page numbers below refer to the numbers shown in the book; PDF page numbers are printed page + 10.

## How the book teaches mathematics

The book presents a problem or visual activity first, asks students to explain their reasoning and compare methods, then offers practice and a chapter review. Students should use diagrams, number lines, tables, physical models, estimation, and written calculations. A correct answer alone is not the whole task: explaining *why* a method works is part of learning.

## Chapter 1 — Number Writing and Patterns

### 1. Review of number writing and numerical calculations (pp. 2–5)

- Read and write whole numbers using place value. Each position is ten times the position immediately to its right.
- Group digits in periods of three, such as ones, thousands, millions, and billions, to read large numbers clearly.
- The value of a digit depends on its position. In `4,205,018`, the digit `2` represents `200,000`.
- Compare whole numbers by checking the number of digits, then comparing digits from left to right.
- Estimate before calculating and use inverse operations to check an answer.
- Solve practical problems involving money, quantities, and multi-step arithmetic. Identify what information is needed before calculating.

### 2. Introducing one billion (pp. 6–9)

- `1,000,000,000` is one billion, or one thousand million.
- A place-value chart extends from millions to hundred millions and billions.
- Multiplying by ten moves each digit one place to the left; dividing by ten moves each digit one place to the right, when viewed in a place-value chart.
- Practice writing very large numbers in digits and words, comparing them, and completing patterns involving powers of ten.
- Use real-world scales, such as population and very large counts, to develop a sense of magnitude.

### 3. Adding and subtracting compound quantities (pp. 10–13)

- A compound quantity combines units, such as hours, minutes, and seconds.
- `1 hour = 60 minutes` and `1 minute = 60 seconds`. These quantities use groups of 60, unlike ordinary base-ten place value.
- Add or subtract corresponding units, then exchange 60 smaller units for one larger unit as needed.
- To subtract when the smaller unit is insufficient, regroup one larger unit: for example, exchange one minute for 60 seconds.
- Interpret clock times and elapsed-time problems carefully; distinguish a time of day from a duration.

### 4. Patterns (pp. 14–17)

- Continue numerical and geometric patterns by finding a consistent rule.
- Record shape number and associated quantities, such as number of tiles or perimeter, in a table.
- Describe how a quantity changes from one step to the next, then use the rule to predict later steps.
- Check whether a proposed rule fits several known terms; one matching term is insufficient evidence.
- Explain a rule in words and, where appropriate, as a numerical expression.

### Chapter review (pp. 18–21)

The review combines large numbers, place value, arithmetic, compound units, numerical and geometric patterns, estimation, and multi-step word problems. Students are asked to explain their reasoning as well as calculate.

## Chapter 2 — Fractions

### 1. Fractions greater than one (pp. 22–26)

- A fraction can be greater than one when its numerator exceeds its denominator; for example, `7/4 = 1 3/4`.
- A mixed number combines a whole number and a proper fraction.
- Convert a mixed number to an improper fraction: `a b/c = (a × c + b)/c`.
- Convert an improper fraction to a mixed number by dividing the numerator by the denominator; the quotient is the whole part and the remainder is the fractional numerator.
- Show both forms with diagrams and on a number line. The *whole* must stay consistent when comparing shaded diagrams.
- Compare and order fractions with common denominators, common numerators, equivalent fractions, or a number line.

### 2. Adding and subtracting mixed numbers (pp. 27–31)

- Add or subtract whole parts and fractional parts, using a common denominator for unlike fractions.
- Regroup when the fractional sum reaches or exceeds one whole, or when subtraction needs to borrow one whole.
- An alternative is to convert mixed numbers to improper fractions before operating.
- Example: `2 1/3 + 1 1/6 = 3 1/2`.
- Check the result with a diagram or estimate; for subtraction, adding the difference back should recover the original quantity.

### 3. Multiplying fractions (pp. 32–35)

- Multiplication can represent repeated groups, scaling, or finding a fraction of a quantity.
- For whole number `n`, `n × a/b = (n × a)/b`.
- For fractions, `(a/b) × (c/d) = (a × c)/(b × d)`.
- A diagram divided in two directions can show why the numerators and denominators multiply.
- Simplify factors before or after multiplying. Check whether a product less than one or greater than one makes sense from the factors.

### 4. Dividing fractions (pp. 36–39)

- Division may mean sharing equally or finding how many groups fit in a quantity.
- Use concrete sharing, drawings, and number lines before applying a symbolic rule.
- For nonzero `c/d`, `(a/b) ÷ (c/d) = (a/b) × (d/c)`.
- Explain why the reciprocal method matches the sharing or measurement interpretation.
- Check division by multiplying the quotient by the divisor.

### 5. Multiplying mixed numbers (pp. 40–43)

- Find an area or another product using a rectangle partitioned by whole and fractional parts.
- Convert each mixed number to an improper fraction and multiply, or distribute over the partitioned parts.
- Example: `1 1/2 × 2 1/3 = 3 1/2`.
- State the unit of a result, especially when multiplying two lengths to obtain area.

### Chapter review (pp. 44–47)

The review asks students to compare fractions, represent division and sharing, work with all four operations, and write explanations. Fraction and mixed-number calculations are embedded in real-life contexts rather than practiced only as isolated expressions.

## Chapter 3 — Ratios, Proportions, and Percentages

### 1. Ratios (pp. 48–51)

- A ratio compares two quantities in a specified order. `3:5` means three units of the first quantity for every five units of the second.
- Distinguish part-to-part ratios from part-to-whole ratios. If three of eight tiles are red, red to all is `3:8`, while red to non-red is `3:5`.
- Write a ratio in words, colon notation, or fraction form when appropriate.
- Simplify ratios by dividing both quantities by a common factor, while preserving their order.
- Ratios can describe ingredients, coloured regions, distances, and group sizes.

### 2. Equivalent ratios (pp. 52–55)

- Multiplying or dividing both parts of a ratio by the same nonzero number makes an equivalent ratio.
- Equivalent ratios describe the same relative amount, although the absolute quantities may differ.
- For example, `2:3 = 4:6`; a larger container with the same mixture ratio contains more of each ingredient.
- Use drawings and tables to compare ratios. Do not infer equal absolute amounts merely from equal ratios.

### 3. Proportion (pp. 56–59)

- A proportion states that two ratios are equal: `a:b = c:d`.
- Find missing values by scaling both quantities, using an equivalent-ratio table, or reasoning about a unit rate.
- Example: If 2 litres of a mixture contain 6 grams of a substance, 4 litres contain 12 grams when the relationship stays proportional.
- Confirm that the situation is proportional before applying a proportion; a fixed starting amount would break direct proportionality.
- Use proportion in measurement, recipes, and comparable geometric figures.

### 4. Percentage (pp. 60–63)

- A percentage is a ratio out of one hundred: `25% = 25/100 = 1/4`.
- Convert a fraction or ratio to an equivalent fraction with denominator 100 when convenient.
- Find a percentage of a whole by multiplying the whole by the percentage as a fraction: `20% of 150 = 30`.
- Interpret percentages in school, environment, agriculture, shopping, and charts.
- Distinguish the part, the whole, and the percentage; the same part has different percentages for different wholes.

### Chapter review (pp. 64–67)

The review links part-to-part and part-to-whole ratios, equivalent ratios, proportional tables, and percentage calculations. It expects students to explain what each comparison means in context.

## Chapter 4 — Symmetry and Polygons

### 1. Line symmetry (pp. 68–72)

- A line of symmetry divides a figure into mirror-image halves.
- Reflect a point by placing its image the same perpendicular distance on the other side of the line.
- Folding paper, transparent overlays, and squared grids help test reflections.
- Identify lines of symmetry and count them for familiar shapes; some shapes have none, one, several, or infinitely many.
- Distinguish an approximately balanced drawing from an exact mathematical reflection.

### 2. Central symmetry (pp. 73–74)

- A half-turn of `180°` around a point maps a centrally symmetric shape onto itself.
- Corresponding points lie on opposite sides of the centre at equal distances.
- Trace and rotate a figure to test whether it has central symmetry.
- Compare central symmetry with line symmetry: one concerns a half-turn, the other a reflection.

### 3. Angles and angle bisectors (pp. 75–78)

- Identify acute, right, obtuse, and straight angles; measure angles in degrees.
- An angle bisector divides an angle into two equal angles.
- The interior angles of a triangle add to `180°`. Cutting out and arranging the three corners gives a visual explanation.
- A straight angle is `180°` and a full turn is `360°`.
- Use known angle sums to find unknown angles and explain each step.

### 4. Polygons and angle sums (pp. 79–83)

- A polygon is a closed figure made of straight line segments. Identify sides, vertices, and interior angles.
- Classify and compare quadrilaterals using properties such as parallel sides, equal sides, and right angles.
- Divide a quadrilateral into two triangles to see that its interior angle sum is `360°`.
- A polygon with `n` sides can be divided from one vertex into `n − 2` triangles; its interior angle sum is `(n − 2) × 180°`.
- Examine tiling and geometric designs as practical contexts for polygon properties.

### Chapter review (pp. 84–87)

The review combines symmetry tests, construction of reflections, angle reasoning, quadrilateral properties, and explanations of angle-sum results.

## Chapter 5 — Decimal Numbers

### 1. Decimal numbers (pp. 88–92)

- Decimal place value extends to tenths, hundredths, and thousandths.
- `0.1 = 1/10`, `0.01 = 1/100`, and `0.001 = 1/1000`.
- A decimal can be shown on a number line, with money or metric measurement, or as a fraction whose denominator is a power of ten.
- Compare decimals by aligning place values; trailing zeros do not change value, so `0.7 = 0.70`.
- Read `2.35` as two and thirty-five hundredths, or two point three five.
- Estimate which whole numbers or tenths a decimal lies between.

### 2. Adding decimals (pp. 93–95)

- Align decimal points so ones, tenths, hundredths, and thousandths are added to like places.
- Add trailing zeros when useful: `1.8 + 3.4 = 5.2` and `1.80 + 3.40 = 5.20`.
- Use a number line, expanded form, or column addition to explain the result.
- Estimate first to notice errors in decimal placement.

### 3. Subtracting decimals (pp. 96–97)

- Align place values and regroup across decimal places when needed.
- Appending zeros is allowed: `3.6 − 2.9 = 3.60 − 2.90 = 0.70`.
- Check by adding the difference to the smaller number.
- Apply subtraction to lengths, masses, fuel use, and other measurements.

### 4. Multiplying decimals (pp. 98–101)

- Multiplication by a whole number can be viewed as repeated addition; `5 × 0.7 = 3.5`.
- Use area models or fractions with denominators 10 and 100 to understand decimal-by-decimal multiplication.
- Multiply as whole numbers and then account for the total number of decimal places in the factors.
- Example: `0.25 × 0.4 = 0.10`.
- Estimate the size of the product before using the written algorithm.

### Chapter review (pp. 102–105)

The review combines decimal notation, comparison, all introduced operations, estimation, measurement, and explanations of place-value alignment.

## Chapter 6 — Measurement

### 1. Area of a rhombus and trapezoid (pp. 106–109)

- A rhombus has four equal sides. Its diagonals divide it into triangles.
- Rhombus area: `A = (diagonal 1 × diagonal 2) / 2`.
- A trapezoid has a pair of parallel sides, called bases. Its height is the perpendicular distance between the bases.
- Trapezoid area: `A = ((base 1 + base 2) × height) / 2`.
- Derive these formulas by cutting and rearranging shapes or comparing them with a rectangle or parallelogram.
- Give area in square units, such as `cm²` or `m²`.

### 2. Circumference of a circle (pp. 110–112)

- Identify the centre, radius, diameter, and circumference. The diameter is twice the radius.
- Measure around a circular object to understand circumference.
- The ratio of circumference to diameter is approximately `π` (about `3.14`).
- `C = πd = 2πr`.
- Use a polygon with many sides to approximate the circle's boundary and understand why finer approximations improve the estimate.
- Record length units for circumference; it is a distance, not an area.

### 3. Volume (pp. 113–117)

- Volume describes the space an object occupies.
- Count unit cubes to compare and measure solid figures.
- One cubic centimetre is a cube measuring one centimetre on each edge.
- For a rectangular prism, `V = length × width × height`; equivalently, multiply the area of one layer by the number of layers.
- Different shapes may have the same volume if they contain the same number of unit cubes.
- Use cubic units such as `cm³` and `m³`.

### 4. Capacity (pp. 118–121)

- Capacity is how much a container can hold. It differs from the amount of liquid currently inside it.
- Informal measures such as “one bowl” can be ambiguous because bowls differ in size; standard units enable fair comparison.
- Use litres and millilitres: `1 L = 1,000 mL`.
- Connect liquid capacity with volume: `1 mL = 1 cm³` and `1 L = 1,000 cm³`.
- Estimate, compare, and convert capacities in practical contexts.

### Chapter review (pp. 122–125)

The review asks students to explain area formulas, compute circle circumference, reason about volume and capacity, and choose appropriate units.

## Chapter 7 — Data and Probability

### 1. Collecting and displaying data (pp. 126–129)

- Pose a question, collect responses, and organise them in a data table.
- Display data with suitable charts, including bar charts and line graphs.
- Label the title, axes, categories, units, and scale. Check that chart heights accurately match the table.
- Compare frequencies, identify the most and least common values, and interpret what the display actually supports.
- Percentages can describe how many observations fall in a category out of the total.
- A line graph is useful when tracking change across ordered times or stages.

### 2. Mean (pp. 130–133)

- The arithmetic mean is an equal-share value for a data set.
- `mean = sum of values ÷ number of values`.
- Example: The mean of `4, 6, 8` is `(4 + 6 + 8) ÷ 3 = 6`.
- Use counters or blocks to show how unequal stacks can be rearranged into equal stacks while preserving the total.
- Keep the unit in the answer. Check whether an extreme value changes the mean substantially.
- Find an unknown value when the mean and other values are known by working backward from the total.

### 3. Probability (pp. 134–137)

- Describe events as impossible, unlikely, equally likely, likely, or certain when those descriptions fit the situation.
- An event's probability lies between `0` and `1`, inclusive: impossible has probability `0`; certain has probability `1`.
- In equally likely simple outcomes, `probability = favourable outcomes ÷ all possible outcomes`.
- Use bags of coloured buttons, spinners, or other simple experiments to compare predicted and observed results.
- Repeated trials can show variation; a likely event need not happen on every trial.
- Be careful when outcomes are not equally likely.

### Chapter review (pp. 138–141)

The review combines reading and creating displays, calculating and interpreting means, and describing likelihood. Students are prompted to explain why a particular chart or probability statement is appropriate.
